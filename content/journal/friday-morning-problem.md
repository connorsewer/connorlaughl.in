The AI productivity story usually has a clean ending.

A company gets smaller. Output goes up. Margins improve. The CEO says the team is flatter, faster, more focused. Analysts nod. The stock moves. A chart appears somewhere with an arrow that has been waiting its whole life for this moment.

Then the story ends.

For the market, that is enough. The event has been translated into price. The company has become more efficient. The future cash flows have been reimagined by people using expensive nouns.

But the human story starts the next morning.

That is the part I keep getting stuck on.

Friday morning. Slack is gone. Email is gone. The laptop may still be there, which is almost worse. A familiar machine with unfamiliar silence inside it. The calendar has emptied itself. The health insurance clock is running. The severance is real and helpful and still nowhere near the size of the hole.

A job is income. It is also proof of place.

That sounds sentimental until the proof disappears.

The AI conversation has a hard time holding this because it likes clean categories. Optimists talk about productivity and abundance. Doom people talk about mass displacement and collapse. Operators talk about workflows. Investors talk about margin. Policy people talk about reskilling, a word that always sounds to me like it was invented by someone who has never had to update a resume at 46 with a mortgage and 2 kids.

The person waking up on Friday morning has a simpler question.

What do I do now?

I do not mean the tactical version. Apply here. Network there. Update LinkedIn. Learn the tool. Take the course. Send the note. Fine. Those things matter.

I mean the deeper question underneath it. What story do I tell myself about the value of my work if the market just celebrated the removal of people who do work like mine?

That is not a productivity question. That is a dignity question.

This is where I get frustrated with the tone of a lot of AI commentary, including some of the commentary I agree with technically. It treats the human cost as a paragraph near the bottom. A caveat. A tradeoff. Something to acknowledge before returning to the fun part, where the machines write code and the org chart gets lighter.

The caveat is the thing.

I am not anti-AI. I build with these tools. I believe smaller teams can do more with them. I believe a lot of work will change, and some of that change will be good. I have seen AI remove drudgery, speed research, help with first drafts, make workflows less brittle, and give a small team reach it did not have before.

I also think the people who benefit from that reach have an obligation to stay awake to what it costs.

There is a temptation to hide inside inevitability. The tools are here. The market is moving. The math is the math. If a company can do more with fewer people, someone will do it. If someone does it and gets rewarded, everyone else gets pressure to follow.

All of that may be true.

It is also incomplete.

Inevitability is often how powerful people launder choices into weather. The storm is coming. Nothing to be done. Better buy an umbrella. Then you look closer and realize somebody opened the dam.

The Friday morning problem is the part of the story that refuses to become weather.

A person has to tell their spouse. A person has to decide whether to cancel a trip. A person has to explain to a kid why they are home. A person has to go from being needed on Thursday to being optimized out by Friday, and then somehow turn that into a professional narrative by Monday.

That is an absurd thing to ask of people.

And yet people do it. They always have. They absorb the shock, make the calls, rewrite the resume, find the next thing, rebuild a little private faith. The resilience of ordinary people is one of the reasons the system gets away with so much.

I do not have a clean policy answer. I distrust clean answers here. The problem is bigger than one company, one CEO, one technology cycle, one severance package.

But I know the first step is refusing to let the story end at the stock chart.

The chart is one instrument. It measures what it measures.

Friday morning measures something else.
