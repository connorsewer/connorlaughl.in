This is a slightly embarrassing confession in the age of burnout discourse, but I still like work.

I do not mean the corporate pageantry around work. I do not like the fake urgency, the calendar Tetris, the soft tyranny of status updates, the little green dot that turns adulthood into a surveillance product.

I mean the work itself.

The thing you make. The problem you sit with. The sentence you revise until it stops lying. The messy system you map on a whiteboard while 3 people realize they have been using the same word to mean 4 different things. The dashboard that finally shows the leak. The handoff that gets cleaner. The team that starts to trust the cadence because the cadence has earned it.

I like that.

This can be hard to say without sounding like a person who owns too many quarter-zips. The internet has flattened professional enthusiasm into 2 bad options: hustle goblin or disengaged ironist. You are either posting sunrise laptop photos about grinding while the world sleeps, or you are performing contempt for the whole machine.

I do not fit cleanly in either camp.

I think a lot of work is organized badly. I think companies ask too much and understand too little. I think AI is about to make the gap between productivity and dignity much uglier unless people with power decide to care. I think plenty of jobs are held together by fear, debt, health insurance, and the faint hope that the next reorg will hit a different floor.

I also think good work can give a day shape.

It can give people a place to practice competence. It can create pride, friendship, memory, usefulness. It can make a person feel less abstract to themselves. There is a reason losing a job hurts in places severance cannot reach. The paycheck matters. Of course it matters. But work also carries identity, rhythm, witness, and the quiet comfort of being expected somewhere.

That is why I get uneasy when companies talk about labor like a removable cost layer.

A role is never only a role to the person inside it. It is rent, insurance, school pickups, confidence, routine, social life, a reason to iron a shirt, a little story about who they are becoming. When the role disappears, the spreadsheet captures the savings and misses the blast radius.

The blast radius is where the human story lives.

This is also why I care about systems. That may sound like a strange turn, but it is not. Bad systems make work humiliating. They create confusion, rework, hidden labor, pointless urgency, and the slow moral injury of caring about something the organization makes difficult to do well.

Good systems do not make work perfect. Nothing does. But they can remove some of the needless stupidity.

They can make the ask clearer. They can make success less mysterious. They can protect people from randomization by whoever yells loudest. They can make the truth easier to say. They can give a team enough structure to spend its energy on the problem instead of the plumbing.

That is a humanist argument for operations, which is not a phrase I recommend putting on a mug.

But I believe it.

The best systems are acts of respect. They say: your time matters, your attention matters, your work should not depend on decoding an invisible maze every morning.

A clean handoff is respect. A good brief is respect. A reliable source of truth is respect. A dashboard with honest definitions is respect. A meeting that ends with a decision is practically a love language.

This is what I mean when I say I still like work.

I like the version where people can be serious without being consumed, ambitious without being hollowed out, productive without turning into little output machines with dental insurance.

Maybe that sounds naive.

Fine. I have made my peace with being a little naive about the possibility of work being better than its worst incentives.

The alternative is cynicism, and cynicism has terrible build quality. It feels sturdy at first. Then one day you realize it cannot make anything.

I would rather make things.
